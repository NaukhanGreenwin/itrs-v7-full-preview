In detail

How a personal account takeover usually happens

A common pattern starts with information that is already public: an email address from an old data breach, a phone number from a people-search site, a home address from a property record. An attacker uses those to reset a password, or talks a mobile carrier into moving the phone number to a SIM they control and then receives the sign-in codes meant for the owner. From one email account they can reach cloud photos, documents, banking alerts and the family's contacts.

The fixes are specific. Account recovery moves from text messages to passkeys, security keys or an authenticator app. The carrier account gets the extra protection the carrier offers. Old accounts are closed, and family details are removed from the data brokers that will take them down. We also agree a short emergency routine, so everyone knows who to call and what to leave alone.

Questions

Frequently asked questions

What is a SIM-swap and how do we prevent it?

An attacker convinces your mobile carrier to move your number to a SIM they control, then receives your text-message codes. Ask your carrier about the extra protection it offers, such as an account PIN or a port-out restriction, and move important accounts to an authenticator app, passkey or security key so a stolen number is not enough.

Can you remove our personal information from the internet?

Some of it. Many people-search and data broker sites delete records on request, and we file and track those requests. News articles, court records and property registries usually stay public, so we focus on reducing what is easy to find and making accounts harder to abuse.

Is this only for the principal?

No. Spouses, children, parents and close staff are common routes in, because their accounts are linked to the principal's. We cover the household and agree with you who is included.

What should we do if we think an account has been hacked?

Call us before changing anything else. We secure the account from a clean device, sign out other sessions, check recovery details and forwarding, and look at what else was linked to it. If money or extortion is involved, we help you report it to your bank and the police.
