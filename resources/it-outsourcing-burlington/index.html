IT Rapid Support Team

"Outsourcing IT" is a phrase that means very different things to the person selling it and the person buying it. To a provider it usually means a managed agreement with a defined scope. To a Burlington business owner it often means something closer to "this stops being my problem" — which is a reasonable thing to want and a dangerous thing to assume without reading the scope.

This guide sets out what actually transfers when you outsource IT, what stays with you no matter what the contract says, how a helpdesk works in practice on a bad Tuesday, and the specific things worth keeping under your own control. It is written by a provider — IT Rapid Support, based at 7810 Keele St in Vaughan — so weigh the recommendations accordingly. The checklist works against our proposal as readily as anyone else's.

What Outsourcing Actually Transfers

A managed IT agreement moves four things off your desk. First, the day-to-day: password resets, new starters and leavers, email problems, printers, software that stopped working. Second, the maintenance nobody enjoys owning — patching, monitoring, backup checks, licence tracking, hardware lifecycle. Third, the security baseline: multi-factor authentication, endpoint protection, detection and response, and email authentication. Fourth, and least visible until you need it, the escalation depth: someone whose job it is to know what to do at two in the morning when the alternative is you searching the internet in a panic.

That is the honest inventory. Notice what is not in it.

What Never Transfers, Whatever the Contract Says

Ownership of your tenant, domain and data. If a provider owns your Microsoft 365 tenant or holds your domain registration, changing provider stops being a handover and becomes a migration. Confirm in writing that your organisation is the legal owner of the tenant and the domain, that you hold a global administrator account of your own, and that documentation and backup data come back to you in a usable format if the relationship ends.

Accountability for your own data. Under Canadian privacy law the organisation collecting personal information stays accountable for it even when a third party processes it. Outsourcing the work does not outsource the obligation, which is why the contract terms around data handling, breach notification and subcontractors matter more than most people give them time. Our PIPEDA IT compliance checklist covers what that means operationally.

The decisions. A good provider will bring you options, evidence and a recommendation. Which risks your business accepts, what it spends, and when it replaces things are yours. Any arrangement where those choices quietly migrate to the vendor ends badly and usually expensively.

The Helpdesk Question, Answered Properly

For most Burlington businesses the helpdesk *is* the service. Everything else — monitoring, patching, strategy — happens invisibly. What staff experience is whether the person who picks up can fix the thing.

Three questions separate helpdesks that work from helpdesks that log tickets. Is the first response a technician or a dispatcher? A dispatcher takes details and creates a queue position; a technician starts working. Second, what are the real coverage hours, and what happens outside them? "24/7" is sold with identical words by an answering service that emails a ticket for Monday and by an engineer who resolves the problem at midnight. Third, does the same team handle both your systems and your security alerts, or do those split between vendors? The gap between two suppliers is where incidents live for hours longer than they should.

The mechanics of round-the-clock coverage — what it costs, what it is worth, and what to verify — are set out in why a 24/7 IT helpdesk matters.

Fully Outsourced, Co-Managed, or a Hire

Three models, and the right answer depends on facts about your business rather than on which one a provider prefers to sell.

Fully outsourced suits businesses with no internal IT person and no appetite to build one — typically under roughly fifty staff, or larger where IT is not close to the product. You get breadth and 24/7 depth for a fixed monthly cost, and you give up having someone in the building whose whole attention is yours.

Co-managed suits businesses that already have one or two capable internal people who are drowning in tickets or cannot cover holidays, nights and specialties. The internal team keeps ownership and context; the provider supplies helpdesk volume, after-hours coverage and security tooling that is uneconomical to build for one company. The trade-offs are set out in co-managed vs fully managed IT.

Hiring makes sense when IT is genuinely core to what you sell, or when scale makes a full internal team cheaper than an equivalent service. The comparison is rarely as favourable as it looks on salary alone once you count coverage, tooling, licensing and the single-point-of-failure risk of one person — managed IT versus in-house runs the arithmetic.

What Is Included in IT Outsourcing Services in Burlington?

A full outsourcing agreement for a Burlington business normally covers the same core list, and a proposal that leaves items out should say so explicitly. A helpdesk your staff can phone, email or log a ticket with. Monitoring of servers, workstations and network equipment, with alerts going to the provider. Patching of operating systems and applications on a set schedule. Microsoft 365 administration: users, licences, mailboxes, permissions and the security settings a default tenant leaves open. Monitored backups with test restores. And a security layer of multi-factor authentication, endpoint detection and response, email security and firewall management.

Around that core sit the items that vary most between providers: on-site visits, new-office projects, hardware procurement, line-of-business application support and vendor coordination with your phone, internet and software suppliers. Ask which of those are in the monthly fee and which are billed as projects.

How Onboarding Works When You Outsource

Onboarding usually takes a few weeks and follows the same four steps whoever you choose. First, an inventory: every user, device, licence, cloud service, backup and administrator credential, documented. Second, a plan for what must be fixed immediately and what can wait, scheduled around your working hours. Third, the switch-over: monitoring and security agents deployed, administrator access moved into your organisation's name, staff introduced to the helpdesk. Fourth, steady state, with regular reviews of what changed.

The step that causes the most trouble is the handover from an outgoing provider. Request domain, tenant and backup access early, in writing, rather than discovering on the last day that one of them sits in the old provider's name. Our guide on how to switch IT providers covers the handover in more detail.

Pricing Models for Outsourced IT

Three pricing models are common. Per-user pricing charges a monthly amount for each person supported and suits offices where everyone has a similar setup. Per-device pricing charges for each computer, server and network device, which can suit a site with shared workstations or production equipment. Tiered plans bundle a defined scope at a few levels, and the useful question is what moves between tiers. Pure hourly break-fix support is the fourth option, cheaper in a quiet month and expensive in a bad one, because nobody is paid to do the preventive work.

IT Rapid Support uses flat monthly plans for ongoing support, structured on our managed IT plans page, and gives a written figure after a short conversation about what you run. One-off general IT work outside an agreement is billed by the hour, scoped in writing first. Our guide to managed IT support costs explains what drives a monthly figure.

Security Basics Any Outsourcing Agreement Should Cover

Whoever runs your IT should be able to show, not just state, that the basics are in place: multi-factor authentication on every account that can reach email or company data; endpoint detection and response on every computer, with someone reading the alerts; patches applied on a known schedule; backups kept with one copy out of reach of the main network and restored on a schedule; email authentication (SPF, DKIM and DMARC) on your domain; and accounts removed the day a person leaves. Those are also the questions cyber insurance applications and customer security questionnaires ask, so having them documented saves time twice.

Before You Sign

Get these in writing: the full inventory of users, devices, sites and network equipment the price is built on; coverage hours and what changes outside them; what is included versus billed as a project, with three concrete examples; whether security controls are inside the base fee; backup scope, retention and the date of the last successful test restore; tenant and domain ownership; onboarding cost and duration; contract term, notice period and auto-renewal; and what leaving looks like.

None of that is adversarial. A provider who has thought their service through will have the answers ready, and the ones who bristle at the questions are answering a different one.

Frequently Asked Questions

What do IT outsourcing services in Burlington typically include?

A helpdesk for your staff, monitoring and patching of servers and computers, Microsoft 365 administration, monitored and tested backups, and a security baseline of multi-factor authentication, endpoint detection and response and email security. On-site visits, projects and hardware purchases vary more between providers and should be listed in the proposal.

How long does it take to outsource IT for a Burlington business?

Usually a few weeks from agreement to steady state: an inventory, a plan, the switch-over and then ongoing operation. The timeline depends mostly on how quickly the outgoing provider hands over domain, tenant and backup access.

Can a Burlington business outsource only part of its IT?

Yes. Co-managed IT keeps your internal IT person in charge of the business-specific work while the provider covers the helpdesk volume, after-hours support, monitoring and security tooling. Some businesses outsource only security or only after-hours coverage to begin with.
