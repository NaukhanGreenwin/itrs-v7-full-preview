The guide

Overview

Queen Street South in Streetsville and Lakeshore Road in Port Credit are the two stretches of Mississauga that still feel like main streets: restaurants, cafes, salons, boutiques, dental and physio clinics, brokerages, accountants and the small offices above them. Most of these businesses run on a point of sale terminal, a handful of laptops, Microsoft 365 or a Gmail account, a Wi-Fi router from the internet company and one person who fixes things when they break, often a relative or a friend of the owner. This guide is about knowing when that arrangement has stopped being enough, and what the next step actually costs and involves.

When the nephew stops being enough

There is nothing wrong with informal IT help while a business is small. The problems arrive quietly. The POS goes down on a Saturday afternoon and the person who set it up is at a cottage. Nobody knows the password to the router, the domain registrar or the Wi-Fi. A laptop with the only copy of the bookkeeping dies. An email arrives from a supplier asking to update banking details and the payment goes to a stranger. None of these are dramatic hacks. They are the ordinary failures of a setup nobody is responsible for.

Statistics Canada's survey of cyber security incidents in 2023, released October 21, 2024, gives a sense of how common the ordinary failures are. About one in six Canadian businesses was affected by an incident that year, scams and fraud were involved in half of them, and small businesses spent roughly $300 million recovering. Only 26% of businesses had a written cyber security policy and 22% carried cyber insurance. Among businesses without cyber security staff, the most common reason given was that they used consultants or contractors instead, which is precisely the move this article describes.

The signals that it is time are practical. You have more than five staff, or more than one location. A day without your systems would cost you money you would notice. You take card payments, keep customer records or hold anything a privacy regulator would care about. Your insurer or a large customer has started asking security questions. Or you simply cannot get hold of the person who knows how it all works.

POS and payments

For a restaurant or a shop the POS is the business. It needs its own network segment so that a customer on the guest Wi-Fi or a staff member's phone cannot reach it, a battery backup so a power flicker does not drop the terminal mid order, and an internet failover, usually a cellular backup, so a cable outage on Lakeshore does not mean cash only for the evening. Card terminals should be on supported firmware, and the POS vendor's support number should be somewhere other than one person's phone. If your vendor hosts the system in the cloud, ask what works offline and for how long. Restaurants and cafes can run through the full list in our IT and cybersecurity checklist for restaurants.

Microsoft 365, set up properly

Most small businesses in Streetsville and Port Credit already pay for Microsoft 365 or should. The difference between paying for it and using it well is a few hours of configuration that rarely gets done: email on your own domain rather than a generic address, multifactor authentication on every account, shared mailboxes for info@ and bookings@ so the business is not locked to one person's login, OneDrive and SharePoint so files live somewhere other than a single laptop, and licences matched to what people actually use. Our Microsoft 365 security best practices guide covers the settings that matter, and our Microsoft 365 managed services page describes how we run it for clients.

Backups you have actually tested

Microsoft 365 keeps your mail and files available. It is not a backup in the sense that matters when someone deletes a folder in March and you notice in June, or when a ransomware infection encrypts the synced copies. The Canadian Centre for Cyber Security's baseline controls for small and medium organizations list backing up and encrypting data among thirteen fundamentals, and the version that works for a small business is simple: a separate backup of Microsoft 365, an image backup of any computer that runs the POS or the accounting, stored away from the premises, and a restore test on the calendar so you know it works before you need it.

Wi-Fi: three networks, not one

The router that came with the internet service was built to get you online, not to protect a business. A proper small business network has three separate wireless networks: one for staff devices, one for the POS and any printers or cameras, and one for guests that reaches the internet and nothing else. The router's administrator password is changed from the sticker, remote administration is off, firmware is updated, and the guest password is rotated occasionally so last year's contractor is not still on your network. The Cyber Centre's baseline calls this securely configuring devices and establishing basic perimeter defences; on a main street it is the difference between free Wi-Fi being a courtesy and free Wi-Fi being an open door.

What managed IT actually involves

Managed IT means one company is responsible for everything in this article, for a fixed monthly fee, with a helpdesk you can call. In practice that means every computer is monitored and patched, backups run and get tested, security software is watched by people rather than ignored, Microsoft 365 is configured and kept that way, new staff get set up and departing staff get shut off, and when something breaks, someone who already knows your setup fixes it. Our break fix versus managed IT comparison and our guide to managed IT support costs in the Toronto area explain how the pricing works and when it pays for itself. For most main street businesses the honest answer is that it costs less than one bad Saturday.

The small business IT checklist

  • You have a written list of every system, account and password, held somewhere other than one person's memory.
  • Multifactor authentication is on for email and any account that touches money or customer data.
  • The POS sits on its own network segment with battery backup and an internet failover.
  • Staff, POS and guest Wi-Fi are three separate networks with the router locked down.
  • Microsoft 365 and the computers that matter are backed up separately, off site, and a restore has been tested.
  • Every computer is patched automatically and runs monitored security software.
  • Staff know that banking changes from a supplier are confirmed by phone, never by email alone.
  • Departing staff lose access the same day.
  • You know who to call when something breaks, and that number answers on a Saturday.
