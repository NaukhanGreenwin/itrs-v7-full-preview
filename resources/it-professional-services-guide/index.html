What Are IT Professional Services? A Guide for GTA Businesses

IT professional services are project work with a defined scope and an end date: a technology provider implements, migrates, installs or configures something for you, hands it over, and the engagement finishes.

The phrase is used loosely. Software vendors use it for their implementation teams, large integrators use it for consulting and staffing, and managed service providers use it for anything billed outside the monthly agreement. This guide explains what IT professional services usually cover for a small or mid-sized business, how they fit alongside managed IT and consulting, what a well-run project should give you at the end, and what to ask before you sign a statement of work. It is written by IT Rapid Support, a managed IT provider at 7810 Keele St in Vaughan, so read it with that in mind; the advice applies whoever does the work.

What IT Professional Services Usually Include

For a business with somewhere between five and a few hundred staff, IT professional services are mostly the projects that happen once every few years and need more hands, or different skills, than day-to-day support. The common ones are:

  • Cloud and email migrations: moving mailboxes, files and users into Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace, or between tenants after a merger or a change of provider.
  • Network projects: a new firewall, switches, Wi-Fi coverage for a warehouse or a second floor, a site-to-site link to another office, or cabling for new desks.
  • Server and infrastructure work: replacing a server that is out of support, moving a line-of-business application to the cloud, or retiring on-premises hardware altogether.
  • Security projects: enforcing multi-factor authentication, deploying endpoint detection, fixing SPF, DKIM and DMARC on your domain, or working through the findings of a security assessment.
  • Office moves and new sites: internet circuits, network drops, phones, printers and a planned cutover weekend.
  • Device rollouts: imaging and deploying a batch of new laptops, or moving devices into Intune so they can be managed and wiped remotely.

What these have in common is a start, a finish and a deliverable you can check. Nobody should be selling you a migration that never ends.

Professional Services vs Managed IT Services vs IT Consulting

These three are sold under overlapping names, and buying the wrong one is the most common mistake. The simplest way to separate them is by the question each one answers.

In practice the lines blur. A consulting engagement often recommends a project, the project is delivered as professional services, and the result is then run under a managed agreement. That is fine, as long as you know which of the three you are paying for at each stage. Our guide to IT consulting services covers the advice side in more depth, and managed IT services vs an in-house team covers the ongoing side.

Is Project Work Included in a Managed IT Agreement?

Usually not, and this is where surprises come from. Most managed IT agreements cover support and maintenance of what you already have: helpdesk tickets, monitoring, patching, backup checks and routine user changes. Projects that add or replace something, such as a migration, a new network or an office move, are normally scoped separately because their size varies so much from one business to the next.

Read your agreement for the word "project" and for the list of exclusions. If it does not say, ask your provider for a written rule, for example where routine work ends and a project begins. A clear answer protects both sides. Our guide to what a managed IT services contract should include covers scope and exclusions in detail.

What a Good Statement of Work Contains

A statement of work is the document that turns "migrate us to Microsoft 365" into something both parties can hold each other to. Before you sign one, check that it states:

  • The scope in specifics: how many users, mailboxes, sites, devices or servers, and which systems are in and which are out.
  • Assumptions: what the provider is relying on you to supply, such as admin access, a contact who can make decisions, or a vendor who will cooperate.
  • The plan and timing: the order of work, when any downtime falls, and whether cutover happens after hours.
  • Acceptance criteria: how you will both know the project is finished, such as all mailboxes moved, every user signed in with MFA, or a test restore completed.
  • Handover deliverables: documentation, credentials stored in your name, network diagrams and a short list of anything left outstanding.
  • How changes are handled: what happens if the scope grows halfway through, and who must approve it before extra work is done.
  • How it is billed: a fixed quote for the defined scope or time billed by the hour, agreed in writing before work starts.

If a proposal is a single line and a total, ask for the rest before you agree. Vague scope is the most common reason a project runs over.

What You Should Have When the Project Ends

The test of a professional services engagement is whether someone else could support the result without calling the people who built it. At handover you should receive current documentation of what was built or changed, administrator credentials held in your company's name, confirmation that monitoring and backups cover the new systems, and a short note of known issues or follow-up work. If the project was a migration, you should also know where the old data went and when the old system can safely be switched off.

If you are changing providers at the same time, sequence matters. Our guide on how to switch IT providers sets out which accounts and backups to secure before the work begins.

Questions to Ask Before Hiring for an IT Project

  • Who will actually do the work, and will the same person be available after go-live if something breaks?
  • Have you done this type of project for a business of our size, and what went wrong on the last one?
  • What will you need from us, and by when?
  • What happens to the systems after handover: do you support them, does our current provider, or do we?
  • How are changes to scope approved and billed?
  • What is the rollback plan if the cutover fails?

A provider that answers the last question plainly has usually planned the rest.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does IT professional services mean?

It means project-based technology work delivered by an outside provider: implementing, migrating, installing or configuring something, with a defined scope and an end date. It is different from managed IT, which is ongoing support, and from consulting, which delivers advice and a plan rather than the finished work.

What is the difference between professional services and managed services in IT?

Professional services are one-time projects that end at handover. Managed services are a continuing agreement to support, monitor and maintain your systems. Many businesses buy both: a project to put something in place, then a managed agreement to keep it running.

Are IT professional services the same as IT consulting?

Not quite. Consulting answers what you should do and produces an assessment, a design or a roadmap. Professional services carry out the work. Some engagements include both, so check which one the proposal actually commits to delivering.

How are IT professional services billed?

Either as a fixed quote for a clearly defined scope or as time billed by the hour, and in both cases the scope should be agreed in writing before the work starts. A fixed quote is only as good as its scope and assumptions, so read those sections closely.

Can a small business buy IT professional services without a managed IT contract?

Yes. Many projects, such as a Microsoft 365 migration, a new network or an office move, are bought on their own. Make sure the handover leaves you with documentation and admin access in your own name, so whoever supports you afterwards can take over cleanly.

Is "IT professional services" the same as IT support for professional services firms?

No. The search phrase is used both ways. This article covers project-based IT work. If you run a consulting, engineering, architecture or advisory practice and want ongoing support, see our page on IT support for professional services firms.
