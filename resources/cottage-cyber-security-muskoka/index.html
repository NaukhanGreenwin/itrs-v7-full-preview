Cottage Cyber Security in Muskoka: Wealth Makes a Target

Muskoka cottages are not modest places anymore.

Call (289) 582-9930Contact us

Back to all resources

A property on Lake Rosseau or Lake Joseph holds a family's finances, an executive taking board calls from the dock, a boathouse full of smart gear and a Wi-Fi network that the cleaner, the dock contractor and last summer's renters all know the password to. Wealth, a remote location and an unmanaged network are exactly what fraudsters look for. Here is what actually goes wrong at the cottage, what a well run setup looks like, and a ten point checklist for this weekend.

Why are Muskoka cottage owners a target?

Criminals go where the money is, and they no longer need to drive up Highway 11 to find it. The Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre recorded $704 million lost to fraud in 2025 from 27,693 reported victims, and only a fraction of victims ever report. Closer to home, Bracebridge OPP issued a warning in February 2026 after two residents lost thousands of dollars in a single day, one of them to the grandparent scam.

A cottage adds three things a city home does not. It sits empty for weeks, so nobody notices a camera going offline or a strange device joining the network. Its network was set up once by whoever installed the internet and never touched again. And it has a rotating cast of people with access: cleaners, contractors, boat mechanics, guests and renters.

Is your Starlink or cottage Wi-Fi actually secure?

Starlink has changed Muskoka. A connection that used to crawl now carries video calls and 4K cameras, and that same connection is the front door to everything in the building. Many cottages run the Starlink router in bypass mode into a third party router that was configured once and forgotten. Three things fix most of what we see.

First, the router admin password should not be the one on the sticker, and the network name should not tell a passing boat which family owns the place. Second, put every smart device and every visitor on a separate guest network. The Canadian Centre for Cyber Security recommends this for homes because it keeps smart devices and unpatched gadgets away from the laptops and phones that hold your banking and work sessions. Third, firmware. Routers, mesh points, cameras and recorders all ship with known holes that get patched over time, and nobody at the cottage is applying the patches. Turn on automatic updates and have someone check the rest on a schedule.

If the Wi-Fi went in alongside the dish, ask who set the passwords and whether the mesh points in the bunkie and boathouse are on the same firmware as the main unit. Our Starlink installation service in Muskoka does this as standard, and we are happy to review an install somebody else did.

What can go wrong with cameras, smart locks and boat lifts?

Smart locks, cameras, thermostats, leak sensors, boathouse doors and even boat lifts now live on the network, usually the same flat network as everything else. Each one is a small computer with a default password and a cloud account attached. The failures are predictable: a camera system open to the internet with the installer's password still on it, a lock code shared with a contractor two summers ago and never revoked, a camera account protected by a password reused from a site that was breached years ago.

Treat every connected device like a door. It gets its own strong credential, it sits on the guest network, remote viewing goes through the manufacturer's app with two factor authentication on rather than a port opened on the router, and access is removed the day someone's work ends.

How does wire fraud hit cottage purchases and renovations?

This is where the losses get large. A cottage purchase moves hundreds of thousands or millions of dollars between buyer, lawyer and seller, and renovations, docks and boathouse projects move six figure deposits to contractors. Fraudsters watch for both. The pattern the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre calls payment redirection, better known as business email compromise, works the same way every time. A mailbox somewhere in the chain is quietly compromised, the criminal reads along for weeks, and the day a deposit or closing balance is due, an email arrives with updated banking details that look entirely normal. The money is usually gone within hours.

The defence is a habit, not a product. Any change to payment instructions gets confirmed by phone on a number you already had, never one taken from the email. Your lawyer and your contractor should hear your voice before a large transfer, and your own mailbox needs multi factor authentication so it is not the weak link. Where a family office or a business handles the transfers, managed security that watches for mailbox rule changes and impossible logins catches the compromise before the fake invoice arrives.

What do executive impersonation and grandparent scams look like at the lake?

They are two versions of the same trick. In the executive version, an assistant, a controller or a family member gets an urgent message that appears to come from the executive who is, conveniently, at the cottage with poor reception: buy the gift cards, approve this wire. In the family version, the Bracebridge OPP warning describes it exactly: a call convincing a grandparent that a family member is in trouble and needs money now. Voice cloning has made both far more convincing.

Agree a family code word for emergencies, and make it a rule that nobody in the household or the business moves money on the strength of a message alone. Slow it down, hang up, and call back on a number you already know. That thirty second pause is the single most effective control there is.

Can you work securely from the cottage?

Yes, and plenty of Muskoka's executives do it from May to October. The cottage laptop needs the same protection as the office one: full disk encryption, a password manager, multi factor authentication on email, banking and the company's cloud apps, and a VPN or zero trust tool so the company network never depends on the cottage Wi-Fi being clean. Keep work and family devices on separate networks.

Who has your Wi-Fi password?

The physical side matters as much as the digital one. List everyone who knows the Wi-Fi password, has a lock code, can see the cameras or holds a key: cleaners, property managers, dock contractors, the renovation crew, the neighbour who checks the pipes in January, and every family that rented the place last August. Most of them are honest. All of them are a path in if their own phone gets compromised. Give guests and trades the guest network and rotate it each season, give each person their own lock code with an expiry, and change the camera and lock passwords when a renter leaves.

What does a managed security service cover at a cottage?

A managed security service takes the checklist off your plate and keeps it done: every laptop, phone and server covered by monitored endpoint detection and response, a firewall and network built and maintained rather than left to whoever plugged in the dish, multi factor authentication enforced, email security that spots the payment redirection attempt, tested backups, patching on a schedule, and people watching the alerts around the clock so that a login from another country at three in the morning gets a phone call rather than a discovery weeks later. For a family with a cottage, a city home and a business, one team can cover all three.

The 10-point cottage security checklist

1. Change the router admin password and the network name, and turn off remote management unless someone you trust is managing it.

2. Put cameras, locks, thermostats, TVs and every guest on a separate guest network, and rotate that password every season.

3. Turn on automatic firmware updates on the router, mesh points and cameras, and have someone check the rest twice a year.

4. Give every smart device and cloud account its own strong password, kept in a password manager, with two factor authentication on.

5. Confirm any change to payment details by phone on a known number before you move money, on cottage purchases, renovations and dock work alike.

6. Agree a family code word for emergency calls, and a rule that nobody moves money on a message alone.

7. Encrypt every laptop and phone that comes to the cottage, and use a VPN or zero trust access for work.

8. Keep a written list of who holds keys, codes, camera access and the Wi-Fi password, and revoke access the day it is no longer needed.

9. Back up the cottage computers and the camera recordings somewhere off the property, and test that the backup restores.

10. Have someone monitor it. A network nobody watches is a network nobody defends.
