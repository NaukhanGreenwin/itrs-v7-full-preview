Overview

Manufacturing IT for plants and distributors in Vaughan has to keep three things running at once: the ERP or MRP system that plans production and ships orders, the plant floor and warehouse equipment that depends on the network, and the office systems that bill for it. We run all three from our office at 7810 Keele St in Concord, inside the industrial district most of our manufacturing neighbours work in, with a 24/7 helpdesk and on-site dispatch when a problem needs hands on a switch, a scanner or a shop-floor PC.

Where Vaughan's manufacturers and distributors actually are

Vaughan is one of the GTA's main industrial cities, and its plants and warehouses are not spread evenly. Concord is the old core: the blocks around Keele Street, Jane Street and Highway 7, and the rail-served lands around CN's MacMillan Yard, are full of fabricators, food processors, printers, building-products suppliers and distributors in buildings that have been extended several times. The Highway 7 and Highway 400 corridor carries newer distribution and light-industrial space with direct highway access. Further south, the Keele and Steeles area runs into the North York industrial belt, and in the west the Pine Valley and Highway 27 corridor is heavily logistics, with large cross-dock and warehouse buildings serving the 400-series highways and the airport.

West of Highway 27, the Vaughan Enterprise Zone is the city's main area of newer employment land along the Highway 427 extension, where larger distribution and production buildings are going up. The IT problems are similar across all of these areas, but the buildings are not. An older Concord plant tends to have a network that grew one switch at a time; a new Highway 27 or Enterprise Zone warehouse has the opposite problem, with a large floor plate, high racking and a Wi-Fi design that was never tested with forklifts and full shelves. Our office is on Keele Street in Concord, so all of these are a same-city drive for a technician.

Plant-floor and OT network segmentation

The most common technical weakness we find in manufacturing environments is a flat network. Office PCs, the ERP server, shop-floor terminals, PLC programming laptops, CNC controllers, IP cameras, badge readers and a machine builder's remote-access box often sit on one network, because each was plugged in when it arrived. That works until one device is compromised, at which point everything else is reachable from it. Ransomware that would have encrypted a few office PCs ends up on the machine that runs the line.

Segmentation is the fix, and it is a planned project rather than a product. We inventory what is connected, group devices by function (office, production, warehouse, building systems, guest), put each group in its own VLAN behind firewall rules that allow only the traffic each system actually needs, and document it so the next equipment install does not undo the work. Production equipment that cannot be patched, such as an older controller running an unsupported operating system the machine vendor still certifies, is fenced in rather than left exposed. Our network security services page describes the firewall and switching side of this in more detail.

We are an IT provider, not a controls integrator. We do not program PLCs or modify machine logic, and we will work alongside your machine builders and integrators rather than around them. What we own is the network those machines sit on and the boundary between them and the rest of the business.

ERP and MRP uptime, shop-floor PCs and EDI

When the ERP or MRP system stops, a manufacturer cannot release work orders, pick orders or print shipping documents, so the cost of an outage is measured in idle shifts and missed trucks rather than inconvenience. Whether your system runs on a server in the building, in a hosted environment or as a cloud service, the parts we look after are the same: the server or virtual machine and its storage, database maintenance and backups taken in a consistent state, the network path from the floor to the system, and the workstations and thin clients people use to reach it. When a problem sits between your ERP vendor's application and our infrastructure, we get on the call with the vendor instead of handing you a phone number.

Shop-floor PCs deserve their own plan. They are usually the oldest machines in the building, shared by several people across shifts, attached to a label printer, a scale or a scanner, and difficult to take offline for updates. We keep an inventory, patch them on a schedule that fits production, lock them down to the applications they need, and plan replacements before they fail. Many still ran Windows 10, which is now past end of support, so that schedule matters.

Distributors and suppliers to large retailers and manufacturers also live on EDI. Purchase orders, advance ship notices and invoices move automatically between your system and your customers' through a translator, a VAN or an EDI provider. When that link fails quietly, orders are missed and chargebacks follow. We monitor the servers, connections and certificates the EDI process depends on and coordinate with your EDI provider when something breaks; the trading-partner maps themselves stay with whoever manages them today.

Warehouse Wi-Fi, handheld scanners and label printers

Warehouse wireless fails in ways office wireless does not. Steel racking and stock absorb and reflect signal, coverage changes as shelves fill and empty, and handheld scanners need to roam between access points without dropping a session halfway down an aisle. A network that tested fine in an empty building can be unusable in peak season. We survey and design coverage for the racked areas, loading docks and yard, set up separate wireless networks for scanners, staff devices and guests, and monitor the access points so a failed unit is noticed before the pickers notice it.

Handheld scanners, mobile computers, label and thermal printers and the warehouse management system they talk to are part of the standard support scope, not an add-on. So is overnight coverage: if your warehouse runs a second or third shift, the IT helpdesk in Vaughan answers then too, with a technician who can connect and fix the problem rather than take a message.

Supplier and vendor remote access

Manufacturers depend on outside parties having access: machine builders who support their equipment remotely, ERP consultants, EDI providers, the security and building-systems contractors, and sometimes a parent company or a customer's quality team. Over the years those arrangements pile up as always-on remote-desktop tools, shared passwords and port forwards that nobody remembers approving. They are one of the most common ways into an industrial network.

We replace that sprawl with controlled access: named accounts with multi-factor authentication, access limited to the specific machines a vendor supports, sessions that are enabled when needed and logged, and a register of who has access to what that you can review. When a vendor relationship ends, their access ends the same day.

Backups and ransomware risk for manufacturers

Manufacturing is a frequent target for ransomware because attackers know that a stopped plant creates pressure to pay quickly. The defences are the same ones we apply to any business, adjusted for an environment that runs around the clock: managed endpoint detection and response on every server and workstation including the shop-floor machines, multi-factor authentication on email and remote access, a segmented network, and alerts watched 24/7 through our threat detection and response service rather than discovered at the Monday production meeting.

The last line is a backup the attacker cannot reach. We monitor backups rather than assume them, keep an offsite copy that cannot be deleted with the same credentials as the live systems, and run test restores of the systems that matter most, starting with the ERP database and the file shares that hold drawings, specifications and quality records. A recovery plan should say which system comes back first and how long it takes, written down before it is needed. Our guide to ransomware protection for Ontario businesses sets out the sequence.

ISO audits and customer security questionnaires

Suppliers to automotive, food, aerospace and large retail customers increasingly receive security questionnaires as part of vendor onboarding or renewal, and quality systems such as ISO 9001 carry their own expectations for document control and records. Cyber insurers ask similar questions at renewal. They tend to ask the same things: is MFA enforced, are backups tested and offsite, is endpoint protection managed, how is vendor access controlled, is there an incident response plan, and who can prove it.

We help you answer those questions accurately and with evidence: the configuration reports, backup test records, access reviews and written policies an auditor or a customer's security team will ask to see. We support the IT controls behind your audits; we do not certify a business, and we will not fill in a questionnaire with answers the environment cannot back up. Our cyber insurance readiness checklist covers the controls most of these questionnaires have in common.
